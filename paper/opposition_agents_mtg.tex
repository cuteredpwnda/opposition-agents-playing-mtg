\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\emph{Magic: The Gathering} (MTG) combines hidden information, evolving card rules and a variable legal-action set. We present a research framework combining the native \texttt{phase-rs} engine with an ontology, graph-conditioned learning prototypes, decision models and belief-space KL control. The provenance-aware ontology has OWL~2~DL as a modelling target; its actual logical and data-validation boundaries are reported separately \citep{neuburger2026ontology}. An offline prototype derives graph evidence from recorded games and trains latent and action-selection components, while the proposed shared-memory and planner integrations remain to be qualified. Using the earlier protocol-v106 runtime, a bounded Modern feasibility campaign reached a rules-terminal outcome in 35 of 36 games; a separate eleven-arm Commander screen completed one game. These are execution measurements, not comparisons of playing strength. Native randomness is not controlled by the picker seed, and controlled learning, graph-benefit and multiplayer studies remain open.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} neuro-symbolic AI, knowledge graphs, world models, JEPA, stochastic optimal control, partially observable Markov decision processes, LLM agents, self-play, Magic: The Gathering.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reinforcement-learning (RL) research on games has progressed through backgammon \citep{tesauro1995temporal}, chess and Go \citep{silver2017mastering, silver2018alphazero}, StarCraft II \citep{vinyals2019grandmaster}, and no-limit Texas hold'em poker \citep{brown2018superhuman}. Each setting motivates different tools, from temporal-difference learning to self-play and counterfactual regret minimisation. \emph{Magic: The Gathering} (MTG) combines several difficult features (\Cref{tab:complexity}): hidden cards and library order, state-dependent actions, card-specific exceptions and an evolving card pool. New cards can require updates to the rules implementation; this work does not claim that unseen card text is automatically parsed into correct mechanics.

In a single turn a player may sequence spells and abilities, choose targets, declare attackers and respond to triggered effects. These decisions create nested choices whose available actions depend on the position. A policy therefore needs an action representation and masking scheme that can handle variable candidate sets; enumerating long continuations can also be costly.

These properties challenge both fixed-vocabulary policies and unrestricted language-model prompting. An LLM that names a move has no guarantee that it is legal, resolves as expected or supports a useful long-term plan. We instead select among actions exposed by the engine, whose own rules coverage still requires independent qualification.

We investigate a \emph{neuro-symbolic} stack: an external rules engine supplies executable actions, an ontology organises domain knowledge, and learned models and planners can use compressed observations. \Cref{fig:stack} sketches the proposed architecture; its arrows are design targets, not evidence that every integration is active in the reported experiments.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This work makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt]
  \item A scientific framing of MTG as an \emph{open-ended partially observable control problem} whose difficulty comes not only from combinatorics, but from the continual introduction of new symbolic rules through quarterly card releases.
  \item An architecture in which the symbolic/sub-symbolic boundary is stated rather than assumed (\Cref{sec:neurosymbolic}): what is symbolic, what is learned, what crosses in each direction, and what gates the crossing.
  \item An offline \emph{play-to-graph} prototype that separates gameplay-derived associations from curated relations, and a proposed shared-memory evaluation for later agents (\Cref{sec:playtograph}).
  \item A \emph{control-theoretic} reading of MTG decision making: we adopt the result of \citet{kaufmann2026aifcontrol} that discrete Active Inference is belief-space KL control, implement that objective directly, and retain the two legacy expected-free-energy factorisations only as ablation arms (\Cref{sec:aif}).
\end{enumerate}

The ontology that grounds the symbolic layer is a contribution in its own right and is described in a companion paper \citep{neuburger2026ontology}; here we treat it as given and focus on what an agent does with it.

\paragraph{Why these four together.} Engine-authored options constrain executable choices, while graph structure and learned dynamics are candidate sources of strategic context. Imagined continuations can still be inaccurate or illegal, and neither their latency nor their benefit is guaranteed. KL control and the fusion baseline are distinct selectors; activating and comparing their components is an empirical task.

\paragraph{Collective intelligence hypothesis.} Heterogeneous agents could generate trajectories from which provenance-linked associations are derived and made available to later policies. Whether such shared memory improves another agent is a hypothesis; current offline graph interventions do not establish that population-level feedback loop.

\paragraph{Scope and what we do \emph{not} do.} We do not claim tournament-strength play, verified rules coverage, faster learning or graded component benefits. The empirical results in \S\ref{sec:eval} qualify bounded execution and a small offline learning pipeline; controlled strength and component comparisons remain future work.

The framework is released as open source\footnote{\url{https://github.com/cuteredpwnda/opposition-agents-playing-mtg}} and the codebase is the canonical artifact accompanying this report. Section~\ref{sec:related} positions the work against prior literature; \S\ref{sec:theory} formalises the POMDP and the four learning principles (world models, FEP, knowledge-graph priors, constrained-decoding LLMs); \S\ref{sec:arch} walks the architecture top-down; \S\ref{sec:eval} presents the controlled study design and current native evidence; \S\ref{sec:limits}--\ref{sec:future} discuss limitations and next steps.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{World models.} \citet{ha2018world} introduced the V+M+C decomposition --- a vision-VAE, recurrent mixture-density dynamics and a controller trained in imagined trajectories. Successors include Dreamer \citep{hafner2020dream, hafner2023dreamerv3} and the Schmidhuber-line of self-referential systems \citep{schmidhuber2015learning, irie2022neural}. We retain this separation but encode structured game observations with a Set-Transformer and optional symbolic context. A separate JEPA head \citep{lecun2022path, assran2023ijepa} learns action-conditioned next-embedding prediction, avoiding the requirement to generate a complete next-state description. LeWorldModel and the modular \texttt{stable-pretraining}/\texttt{stable-worldmodel} ecosystem provide related methods and reusable research infrastructure \citep{balestriero2025stable, maes_lelidec2026swm, maes_lelidec2026lewm}. Representation prediction is a training principle, not itself a policy or a proof of strategically sufficient abstraction.

\paragraph{Active inference and stochastic optimal control.} The Free-Energy Principle of \citet{friston2010free} and its discrete-state operationalisation \citep{sajid2021active} propose that action selection minimise \emph{expected free energy} (EFE), conventionally decomposed into a pragmatic (reward-seeking) and an epistemic (information-seeking) term. \citet{kaufmann2026aifcontrol} shows that this framing inverts the actual mathematical hierarchy: continuous-time Active Inference on path space \emph{is} finite-horizon path-integral stochastic MPC in the sense of \citet{kappen2005path}, \citet{todorov2009efficient} and \citet{williams2017mppi}, and its canonical discretisation is classical KL control on belief space. The exploration--exploitation trade-off is not supplied by an added epistemic term; it is Fel'dbaum's dual control effect \citep{feldbaum1960dual}, present in stochastic control from the outset, and it is recovered as soon as plans are evaluated \emph{closed-loop} over beliefs rather than as open-loop action sequences. We follow that result rather than the conventional decomposition, for the concrete engineering reasons set out in \Cref{sec:aif}: the two extant EFE factorisations have opposing, empirically demonstrated failure modes, and both are avoidable.

\paragraph{Knowledge graphs, GraphRAG, and their limits.} Neo4j with the \texttt{n10s} plugin enables direct ingestion of OWL ontologies as labeled property graphs \citep{neo4j_n10s}; the APOC library adds graph algorithms; GraphSAGE \citep{hamilton2017inductive} provides inductive node embeddings. GraphRAG \citep{edge2024graphrag} complements vector RAG for queries that benefit from explicit relational structure, and \citet{liao2026graphragbp} give the first component-level ablation of a GraphRAG pipeline, finding that retriever choice and graph \emph{serialisation format} dominate the outcome --- structured, code-like serialisations outperform prose renderings of the same subgraph. MTG is a textbook GraphRAG case: ``find me a two-card combo using only cards in my library that survive \emph{Counterspell}'' is a multi-hop graph query, not a similarity search. Two cautions from the same literature shape our design. \citet{diettrich2026harp} show that consumers of an evolving schema must discover it at runtime rather than assume it, because static pipelines fail silently under schema drift; and \citet{romanova2026relationships} gives a per-relation diagnostic for whether explicit graph structure still adds signal beyond node text at all, which is the honest way to decide where graph engineering is worth the effort.

\paragraph{Foundational ontologies and the symbolic layer.} The intended symbolic substrate is a provenance-aware OWL ontology of MTG aligned to DOLCE-UltraLite \citep{gangemi2002dolce, borgo2022dolce}. Its conceptual schema and tooling were developed with LLM assistance; its normative vocabulary is mechanically derived from the Comprehensive Rules without runtime LLM inference. A companion paper \citep{neuburger2026ontology} separates construction responsibilities and reports logical and data-validation scopes. Constraints are expressed as testable axioms, but schema consistency is not full profile conformance of the imported documents or certification of a populated agent graph. The broader turn toward competency-question-driven, validator-gated ontology construction \citep{garijo2026maseo, cq4oe2026, lippolis2026ontoextend, tiwari2026ontoprompt} is methodological context, not evidence that those external pipelines were executed here.

\paragraph{Neuro-symbolic systems and the curated/induced boundary.} The framing that organises \Cref{sec:neurosymbolic} is the distinction between \emph{curated} and \emph{induced} semantics. \citet{sabou2026neurosymbolic} argues that trustworthy neuro-symbolic AI requires explanations grounded in domain knowledge rather than saliency, and identifies provenance and continuous auditing as the missing infrastructure. \citet{thombre2026selfdemo} demonstrate that neuro-symbolic decomposition --- symbolic constraints narrowing the search space, the neural component reasoning inside each narrowed sub-task --- substantially outperforms monolithic prompting. \citet{vanschependom2026synthology} generate reasoning training data from an ontology by backward-chaining proof search, the natural route to a reasoning benchmark \emph{for} a schema such as ours. \citet{ehrenmuller2026sock} address the reconciliation of conflicting graph claims from heterogeneous sources with explicit methodological provenance. Our offline prototype records provenance for gameplay-derived associations; independent verification of the full engine and graph-writing pipeline remains open.

\paragraph{MTG and other complex card games.} Earlier MTG-AI work has used hand-crafted heuristics \citep{cowling2012ensemble}, MCTS, and small policy nets on restricted card pools (e.g., \texttt{open-mtg}). On the rules side, \texttt{Forge}, \texttt{XMage} and \texttt{Mage} provide JVM-based reference implementations, and \texttt{phase-rs} provides a Rust one. To our knowledge, no prior public system combines a full Comprehensive-Rules engine with a learned world model and a foundational-ontology-grounded symbolic layer. \citet{moravvcik2017deepstack} (poker), \citet{vinyals2019grandmaster} (StarCraft) and \citet{schrittwieser2020mastering} (MuZero) are spiritual antecedents at the algorithmic level.

\paragraph{Ontologies for games.} Game ontologies support purposes including educational metadata, game design and simulation. The companion resource \citep{neuburger2026ontology} targets interoperability between normative vocabulary, card descriptions, contextual gameplay and strategic evidence. Relative coverage and modelling commitments of other MTG representations have not yet been systematically compared; no priority claim is made.

\section{Theoretical Foundations}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{MTG as a partially-observable Markov game}
We model a bounded game from the perspective of a single seat as a finite-horizon POMDP $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{O}, T, \Omega, R, \gamma, b_0)$ embedded inside an $n$-player Markov game. The full state includes public and private zones, the stack, resources and game flags. A controlled-perspective observation hides opponents' unrevealed cards and library order. The engine supplies a variable legal-action set at each decision; the transition kernel also depends on random events and other players' choices. The current bridge does not seed native shuffle or opponent-AI randomness, so the picker seed does not reproduce an entire game. A terminal win/loss reward can be supplemented by auxiliary training signals.

The optimal value function over beliefs $b_t \in \Delta(\mathcal{S})$ satisfies the Bellman equation
\begin{equation}
V^*(b) = \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \left[ \sum_s b(s)\, R(s,a)
   + \gamma \sum_o \Pr(o \mid b, a) \, V^*\!\big(b'_o\big) \right],
\end{equation}
where $b'_o$ is the Bayes update of $b$ after taking $a$ and observing $o$. Exact solution is intractable. The zoo instead contains reactive selectors, learned latent predictors and approximate belief-based planners; they do not all maintain an explicit Bayesian belief or optimise the same objective.

\subsection{World models as latent imagination}
\label{sec:jepa-foundation}

The V+M+C decomposition of \citet{ha2018world} separates representation learning, dynamics prediction and action selection. Our inputs are structured, perspective-filtered game observations: visible card identities and characteristics, zones, resources, turn information and public actions. The relevant choice is \emph{predicting a compact representation of future observations versus predicting a complete next game-state description}.

\paragraph{What is predicted.} An encoder $E_\phi$ maps an observation $o_t$ and optional symbolic context $g_t$ into a latent vector $z_t$. An action-conditioned predictor $f_\theta$ predicts the representation that the encoder would assign to the next observed position:
\begin{equation}
z_t=E_\phi(o_t,g_t),\qquad
\hat z_{t+1}=f_\theta(z_t,a_t),\qquad
z^{\mathrm{target}}_{t+1}=\mathrm{sg}\!\left(E_\phi(o_{t+1},g_{t+1})\right).
\label{eq:jepa-target}
\end{equation}
Here $\phi$ and $\theta$ denote learned parameters, $a_t$ is an encoded action, and $\mathrm{sg}$ means \emph{stop gradient}: the target is treated as fixed for this loss evaluation. This equation describes a shared-encoder target; the native v4 experiment instead maintains an exponential-moving-average target and adds variance regularisation. Training asks whether the consequence of an action is predictable in the learned representation, rather than demanding an exact listing of every future card, flag and hidden zone.

This is the joint-embedding predictive principle \citep{lecun2022path, assran2023ijepa, maes_lelidec2026lewm}. It can allocate capacity to regularities useful for evaluating positions without decoding a full symbolic successor state. That is an inductive bias, not a guarantee: compression may discard a decisive interaction, and unpredictable hidden information does not become observable merely because a latent exists. The learned representation $z_t$ is also distinct from a Bayesian belief $b_t$ over possible hidden states.

\paragraph{What the training loss compares.} The present JEPA objective uses consecutive transitions $(o_t,a_t,o_{t+1})$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{JEPA}}
=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}\!\left[
  \mathrm{MSE}\!\left(\hat z_{t+1},z^{\mathrm{target}}_{t+1}\right)
  +\beta_J\,\KL\!\left(q_\phi(z\mid o_t,g_t)\,\|\,\mathcal N(0,I)\right)
\right].
\label{eq:jepa-loss}
\end{equation}
The replay data $\mathcal D$ contains recorded transitions. The first term measures next-embedding prediction error. The second regularises the encoder's Gaussian distribution
$q_\phi=\mathcal N(\mu_\phi,\operatorname{diag}\sigma_\phi^2)$ against a standard normal reference; $\beta_J$ controls its weight. This is \emph{not} a KL divergence against an LSTM-predicted next-state prior. Nor is it an $H$-step training loss: repeated rollouts are a planning operation, while this objective supervises one step at a time. Gaussian regularisation and stop-gradient define the current training choice; neither alone establishes that representations cannot collapse or that they retain all strategically relevant information.

\paragraph{How predictions become decisions.} Planning starts with a candidate legal action and repeatedly applies a latent transition model to imagine a continuation. Predicted rewards or values score those continuations; their scores are aggregated to compare candidate first actions. A generic predicted-return estimate is
\begin{equation}
\widehat J_H(a)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
 \sum_{\ell=1}^{H}\gamma^{\ell-1}\hat r^{(k)}_{t+\ell}
 \quad\text{with first action }a_t=a.
\label{eq:wm-rollout}
\end{equation}
Here $H$ is planning depth, $K$ the number of sampled continuations, $\gamma$ a discount factor and $\hat r$ a predicted reward. Larger values are preferred. No complete successor state is generated and the rules engine is not called inside these imagined continuations. The real first action must still come from the engine's legal set.

JEPA prediction, reward/value learning and search are separate components. The present dream-search path uses recurrent stochastic dynamics and a predicted-reward head; training the JEPA head does not by itself replace that rollout model. A future JEPA-driven planner must qualify this connection explicitly. Multi-step error, latent collapse, reward accuracy and the legality of imagined continuations therefore remain distinct evaluation questions; a low one-step embedding loss is not evidence of accurate long-horizon play.

\subsection{Active inference as path-integral control}
\label{sec:aif}

We first distinguish \emph{belief estimation} from \emph{action selection}. In the notation of \Cref{sec:theory}, $s$ is the hidden game state and $o$ an observation. A generative model $p_{\mathrm{gen}}(o,s)$ describes how states produce observations; an approximate distribution $q_{\mathrm{bel}}(s)$ represents the agent's current belief. Friston's variational free energy \citep{friston2010free} is
\begin{equation}
F[q_{\mathrm{bel}}]
=\KL\!\left(q_{\mathrm{bel}}(s)\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{gen}}(s\mid o)\right)
 -\log p_{\mathrm{gen}}(o).
\end{equation}
For a fixed observation the evidence term is constant, so minimising $F$ makes the approximate belief closer to the model's posterior. It need not make that posterior less uncertain. This estimation problem does not yet specify which action to take.

\paragraph{Goals, defaults and controlled predictions.} Let $\eta$ denote the game-state coordinates on which goals are evaluated, such as survival, opponent elimination and material advantage. These are properties of a possible game position, not the learned JEPA embedding. A preference distribution $p_{\mathrm{pref}}(\eta)$ specifies which positions are desirable, with cost
\begin{equation}
V(\eta):=-\log p_{\mathrm{pref}}(\eta).
\label{eq:potential}
\end{equation}
Lower $V$ means a more preferred position. One may specify costs directly and read $p_{\mathrm{pref}}\propto e^{-V}$, up to normalisation; the costs are not calibrated win probabilities.

A policy $\pi$ chooses actions from beliefs. Let $\tau$ denote a horizon-length trajectory, including states, actions and observations. $Q^\pi(\tau)$ is its predicted distribution when following $\pi$, accounting for hidden-state uncertainty and future observations. A default policy $q_0(a\mid b)$ instead induces the reference trajectory distribution $P_0(\tau)$. This \emph{passive prior} is a baseline way of acting, not the agent's belief about hidden cards; ``passive'' does not mean that no actions occur.

\paragraph{What a Gibbs tilt means.} Following \citet{kaufmann2026aifcontrol}, define a target trajectory distribution by reweighting the default trajectories:
\begin{equation}
P_V(\tau)=\frac{1}{Z}\,P_0(\tau)\,e^{-V(\eta_T)},\qquad
Z=\mathbb E_{P_0}\!\left[e^{-V(\eta_T)}\right].
\label{eq:gibbs-target}
\end{equation}
This is a \emph{Gibbs tilt}: multiply each baseline probability by an exponential preference weight, then renormalise so probabilities sum to one. With equally probable trajectories and terminal costs 0 and 1, their weights become $1$ and $e^{-1}$, giving approximately $0.73$ and $0.27$. It changes the distribution we want to approach; it does not change the rules of the game.

The control objective measures how far the policy's predicted trajectories are from that target:
\begin{equation}
G(\pi)=\KL\!\left(Q^\pi(\tau)\,\|\,P_V(\tau)\right).
\label{eq:pathefe}
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:gibbs-target} gives an interpretable decomposition:
\begin{equation}
G(\pi)=
 \underbrace{\KL\!\left(Q^\pi\,\|\,P_0\right)}_{\text{deviation from default}}
 +\underbrace{\mathbb E_{Q^\pi}[V(\eta_T)]}_{\text{expected terminal cost}}
 +\log Z.
\label{eq:klcontrol}
\end{equation}
The constant $\log Z$ can be dropped when comparing policies. The first term discourages costly deviations from a specified reference; the second favours good game outcomes. If the controlled and reference processes share transition/observation kernels, their action-conditioned trajectory KL decomposes into expected action-policy divergences, $\sum_t\mathbb E[\KL(\pi(\cdot\mid b_t)\|q_0(\cdot\mid b_t))]$. A deterministic choice of action $a$ at one decision has cost $-\log q_0(a\mid b)$. This does not make all state-only trajectory divergences equal to action divergences: actions can lead to indistinguishable states.

Kaufmann's continuous-time result relates the corresponding path objective to stochastic model-predictive control under its diffusion assumptions. MTG actions are discrete; we use the belief-space formulation rather than claiming the Rust engine is a continuous diffusion.

\paragraph{Information is valuable through subsequent decisions.} Information can be valuable when it lets the agent make better subsequent decisions. A planner that accounts for these contingent decisions can favour useful information-gathering actions without adding a separate information bonus. This interaction between acting and learning is called \emph{dual control} \citep{feldbaum1960dual}. Scrying and drawing cards remain worthwhile when their gameplay benefits outweigh their costs.

For example, casting a less important spell before committing a key threat both advances the position and elicits a response. If the opponent spends a counterspell, that response changes what the agent knows and how it should play next. The information matters because it enables a better follow-up, not simply because uncertainty decreases. In \emph{closed-loop} planning, later actions depend on what is observed; a fixed, open-loop action sequence cannot express that contingency. A short or inaccurate planner can therefore miss useful information. This describes a capability to be evaluated, not a demonstrated property of our current planner.

\paragraph{Why not the conventional factorisations.} The two EFE forms in common use are
\begin{align}
G_{\mathrm{IG}}(\pi) &=
 -I_Q(\eta;o\mid\pi)-\mathbb E_Q[\log p_{\mathrm{obs}}(o)], \label{eq:efe1}\\
G_{\mathrm{AMB}}(\pi) &=
 \KL\!\left(Q(\eta\mid\pi)\,\|\,\tilde p_{\mathrm{pref}}(\eta)\right)
 +\mathbb E_{Q(\eta\mid\pi)}[H[p_{\mathrm{obs\,model}}(o\mid\eta)]].
 \label{eq:efe2}
\end{align}
Here $I_Q$ is expected mutual information (reduction in hidden-state uncertainty), $H$ is Shannon entropy, $p_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is a preference over observations, $\tilde p_{\mathrm{pref}}$ a state preference, and $p_{\mathrm{obs\,model}}$ the observation likelihood. Observation preferences describe desired readings; likelihoods describe how readings arise. The factorisations have distinct assumptions and should not be treated as interchangeable objectives \citep{millidge2021whence, kaufmann2026aifcontrol}.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Information without instrumental value (``noisy TV'').}
  If uncertainty reduction earns an unconditional bonus, an agent can prefer learning facts that do not improve its later decisions. A stylised MTG test would offer repeated library inspection when the information cannot change any useful continuation, while using mana or forfeiting tempo needed to stabilise. Scry, surveil and cantrips are not inherently pathological: they can improve draws, supply cards or enable synergies. A failure is choosing them for the bonus \emph{despite} their total effect on the game objective. Moreover, genuinely independent random noise has zero mutual information with the hidden state; a correctly calculated information-gain term need not reward it. Capture requires, for example, learnable but irrelevant variables or a misspecified novelty/uncertainty proxy. The test must distinguish these cases.
  \item \textbf{Avoidance of uncertain observations (``scotophobia'').}
  The term means an aversion to ``dark'', poorly observed situations. The ambiguity term in \eqref{eq:efe2} penalises entropy of observations \emph{conditional on a state}, not simply the variance of game rewards or uncertainty about an opponent's hand. Confusing these quantities can make informative or tactically favourable lines look costly merely because their feedback is uncertain. As a toy cost comparison, a line giving terminal costs $-4$ with probability $0.75$ and $2$ with probability $0.25$ has expected cost $-2.5$, better than a safe line costing $-1$. With equal action costs, an additional uncertainty surcharge above $1.5$ reverses that ordering. Whether such a surcharge actually arises from the chosen observation model must be tested; unknown blockers or open mana alone do not establish it. Avoiding a risky line may instead be rational under loss-sensitive preferences.
\end{itemize}

These are \textbf{testable failure hypotheses, not observed MTG results}. The legacy ablation selectors use simplified entropy/likelihood proxies, not a validated exact implementation of every observation-channel factorisation. \Cref{sec:eval-aif-ablation} must establish both the proxy's meaning and the behavioural effect before attributing a game outcome to either mechanism.

\paragraph{Soft selection rather than a hard minimum.} On a finite candidate set, let $q_0(\pi)$ be reference probabilities and $J(\pi)$ the predicted task cost of each candidate, before adding the KL regularisation for this selection step. Minimising $\mathbb E_r[J]+\alpha^{-1}\KL(r\|q_0)$ over selection distributions $r$ yields
\begin{equation}
p_\alpha(\pi)=\frac{q_0(\pi)e^{-\alpha J(\pi)}}
 {\sum_jq_0(\pi_j)e^{-\alpha J(\pi_j)}},
\qquad
\operatorname{softmin}_\alpha(J)
=-\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\sum_iq_0(\pi_i)e^{-\alpha J(\pi_i)}.
\label{eq:gibbs}
\end{equation}
This is again exponential reweighting. For equal priors, costs 0 and 1 and $\alpha=2$, the lower-cost option receives probability approximately $0.88$. As $\alpha\to0$ selection approaches the prior; as $\alpha\to\infty$ it concentrates on minimum-cost candidates within the prior's support. The scalar $\alpha$ is inverse temperature (selection precision), distinct from JEPA's $\beta_J$. It is not itself a measured compute budget: rollout count, horizon and wall-clock limits control computation separately. Nor should the same deviation cost be included in $J$ and charged a second time through the KL regulariser.

\paragraph{Where other signals belong.} Heuristic, graph and language-model scores can shape the reference action policy $q_0$: for example, a known combo completion may become a more probable default. The controller then evaluates whether predicted consequences justify following or departing from that default. Prior design still involves choices and calibration; replacing a weighted score sum with KL regularisation does not make these assumptions disappear. Below, the shorter symbol $q$ denotes this action prior, never $q_{\mathrm{bel}}$ or the encoder distribution $q_\phi$. Current native KL pilots use analytic state projections and forecasts, not a trained JEPA transition model, and do not qualify the complete learning architecture.

\subsection{Knowledge graphs as a symbolic prior}
GraphSAGE \citep{hamilton2017inductive} can embed cards from their features and graph neighbourhoods, including suitable unseen nodes. The native prototype trains a graph encoder on separate curated and gameplay-induced relations and supplies graph context to its controller. Using graph retrieval to shape the KL prior $q$ is a proposed extension, not a feature established by the current KL pilots.

The offline study keeps source-derived and induced evidence separate and records provenance for the induced co-presence graph. This supports auditing, but does not demonstrate a shared, append-only live graph queried by all agents or reproducible native game replay.

\subsection{The neuro-symbolic boundary}
\label{sec:neurosymbolic}

``Neuro-symbolic'' is used loosely enough that it is worth stating exactly what is symbolic here, what is learned, and what crosses between them. The system contains three symbolic artefacts and three learned ones, and the interfaces between them are narrow by construction.

\paragraph{Symbolic side.} The external \texttt{phase-rs} engine enumerates actions and executes rules; its full correctness has not been independently verified here. The ontology (\Cref{sec:ontology}) defines a vocabulary and has scoped reasoner and SHACL checks. Separate graph inputs are derived from card and combo data.

\paragraph{Learned side.} The native prototype trains GraphSAGE embeddings, a JEPA predictor, recurrent dynamics and an imitation controller on recorded games against native AI. Language and decision models can directly select among supplied actions; their use as a planner prior remains unqualified.

\paragraph{What crosses, and in which direction.} The crossings are the substance of the architecture:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Symbolic $\rightarrow$ neural (structure as inductive bias).} The native graph intervention supplies graph embeddings to the learned controller, while the engine supplies the executable action list. Learned predictions can still be wrong.
  \item \textbf{Symbolic $\rightarrow$ neural (reference measure).} Heuristic priors are used in the KL picker. Graph-conditioned priors are proposed, but not active in the reported KL experiments.
  \item \textbf{Neural $\rightarrow$ symbolic (induction).} The offline pipeline extracts repeated controlled-seat card co-presence as an attributed association, not as a causal synergy or a normative rule.
  \item \textbf{Validation as a proposed gate.} Promotion of induced evidence into curated relations requires a separately designed and evaluated human-review and validation process. The current schema checks do not certify every generated candidate.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Curated versus induced semantics.} Source-derived facts and learned regularities should remain distinguishable. A co-occurrence statistic is defeasible and can reflect the sampled deck pool. The ontology provides epistemic-status annotations and provenance patterns; their presence in the schema does not by itself prove that every external graph writer enforces them.

\subsection{General LLMs as indexed-action selectors}
The action interface supplies a filtered observation $o_t$ and an indexed engine-authored action list $\mathcal A_t$. A general chat LLM is asked to select an index:
\begin{equation}
\hat\imath=\mathrm{LM}\!\left(\rho(o_t,\mathcal A_t)\right),
\qquad a_t=\mathcal A_t[\hat\imath].
\end{equation}
Only an index resolved to an original engine-provided action can reach the engine. Strict Ollama and local vLLM selectors request a JSON schema over eligible indices and validate the returned object; when a non-concession action exists, concession is excluded. Unavailable services and malformed replies fail explicitly, while legacy random fallback is opt-in. Constrained formatting does not yield a calibrated choice distribution or guarantee tactical correctness.

\subsection{Decision-tuned models as option selectors}
\label{sec:decision-model-foundation}

A \emph{decision model} here is a pretrained language model adapted to choose among supplied alternatives. It is not a world model: it returns a choice, not a predicted successor state or a simulated trajectory. Unlike a general chat selector prompted to emit an integer, its training and API are specialised for categorical decisions. Tev1 is an experimental Qwen3.5-derived family with supervised decision adaptation and a retained language-model output head \citep{together2026tev1, ollama2026tev1}; decision tuning does not by itself establish MTG expertise.

Given a filtered observation $o_t$, a context projection $R(o_t)$ and a subset $\mathcal S_t\subseteq\mathcal A_t$ of engine-authored actions, it supplies
\begin{equation}
d_\psi(a\mid R(o_t),\mathcal S_t),\qquad a\in\mathcal S_t,
\end{equation}
where $\psi$ denotes the selector parameters. The selected option maps back to the original action, preserving targets and object identity. Returned option probabilities indicate the model's preference among the presented alternatives, not a probability of winning or a calibrated probability that a choice is correct.

The candidate generator is part of the policy: if a winning line is absent from $\mathcal S_t$, no reranker can recover it. Likewise, projection $R$ can omit strategically important context. For example, selecting removal from a shortlist can be sensible only if the supplied state distinguishes the relevant threat, available mana and target. Measuring legal output is therefore insufficient; matched-context/candidate comparisons, shortlist coverage, tactical quality and inference cost are separate measurements (\Cref{sec:decision-benchmark}).

There are three possible uses, which should not be conflated: direct action selection, reranking candidates proposed by a planner, and supplying probabilities to the planner's passive prior $q_0$. Only the first is currently integrated for Tev1. None replaces the rules engine or provides the latent transition model needed for JEPA rollouts. Local interface and terminal-game checks pass with a same-weight larger-context deployment; neither establishes calibrated choice quality or a Tev1 playing-strength advantage.

\subsection{Self-play promotion and the native prototype}
The legacy Python training pipeline includes a champion--challenger loop with a configurable promotion threshold, defaulting to a win rate above $0.55$. The reported native learning prototype instead collects heuristic and random trajectories against a fixed native opponent; it does not execute this promotion loop. Neither the finite-match promotion rule nor JEPA validation loss establishes convergence to a Bellman-optimal policy. Self-play improvement remains a future empirical question \citep{tesauro1995temporal, silver2017mastering, silver2018alphazero, schrittwieser2020mastering}.

\subsection{Game engine}
The runtime engine is \texttt{phase-rs} (Rust) \citep{phase2026engine},
published at \url{https://github.com/phase-rs/phase} and accessed over its
WebSocket interface via \path{src/integrations/phase_rs/}.
Our Python side is intentionally adapter-only at execution time:
\texttt{client.py} handles protocol envelopes,
\texttt{runner.py} drives game loops,
\texttt{adapter.py} maps legal actions/snapshots into our policy API,
and \texttt{server\_process.py} manages local server lifecycle for
training scripts.

\paragraph{Tracking a living upstream.} \texttt{phase-rs} is under active
development and its wire protocol is versioned and enforced at handshake.
Over the period covered by this report the upstream protocol advanced from
v6 through v75 and v106 to v374 in the newly pinned checkout. The reported
campaign evidence uses v106. One v374 game each in Standard, Pioneer, Modern,
Legacy and four-player Commander completed, but broader runtime and campaign
qualification remain necessary. This illustrates that an external rules engine is a
dependency with its own release cadence, not a fixed substrate. Two of
those changes were structural rather than additive and required adapter
work: rejections became a typed \texttt{ActionRejection} carrying a stable
machine-readable code and a recovery disposition rather than a prose
string, and the engine now publishes \emph{viewer interactions} --- an
explicit, engine-authored protocol for the choices a player must make
during resolution (targets, modes, damage assignment, ordering). The
second is the more significant for us: it moves a large class of
decisions out of the flat \texttt{legal\_actions} list into a structured
opportunity schema, which is strictly better for an agent that wants to
reason about \emph{what kind} of choice it is making. We pin the protocol
constant in \texttt{client.py} and fail closed on mismatch rather than
attempting to negotiate, on the principle that a silently
misinterpreted game state is worse than a refused connection.

Core integration modules:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=1pt]
  \item \texttt{client.py}: protocol handshake, message parsing, and typed
    message dataclasses for \texttt{GameStarted}, \texttt{StateUpdate},
    and \texttt{GameOver};
  \item \texttt{runner.py}: end-to-end game loop for a Python-controlled seat,
    including structured per-decision JSONL traces;
  \item \texttt{adapter.py}: practical \texttt{GameAction} $\leftrightarrow$
    \texttt{Action} bridge and snapshot projection for native Python
    policies;
  \item \texttt{agent\_bridge.py}: random/heuristic/LLM pickers plus
    adapter-backed native-agent picker support;
  \item \texttt{server\_process.py}: managed local \texttt{phase-server}
    process startup/shutdown for training and evaluation runs.
\end{itemize}
The old Python engine remains in-repository as legacy compatibility code,
but new experiments and reported benchmarks use \texttt{phase-rs} as the
authoritative runtime transition system.

\subsection{Symbolic substrate: the ontology}
\label{sec:ontology}

The symbolic layer is an OWL ontology of MTG derived from the Comprehensive Rules and aligned to the DOLCE-UltraLite foundational ontology, with OWL~2~DL as its modelling target. It is a contribution in its own right and is described in a companion paper \citep{neuburger2026ontology}; we summarise here the intended symbolic contract and its current qualification boundaries.

Three properties matter for the argument of this paper. First, the ontology is \emph{expressive enough to be wrong}: logical analysis exposed incompatible property characteristics that were repaired, and HermiT finds the import-resolved schema consistent with no unsatisfiable named classes. This is not complete OWL~2~DL profile conformance of the raw external documents, nor certification of a populated card graph. The CR~205.3 card-type/subtype correlation is also checked procedurally. Second, its factual vocabulary is \emph{derived, not curated by hand}: 3{,}165 source rules are parsed and 830 vocabulary subjects carry direct document provenance; rule text remains local rather than being redistributed. Third, and most importantly here, it distinguishes \emph{curated} from \emph{induced} assertions at the schema level. That distinction supplies a necessary audit boundary for the learning loop of \Cref{sec:playtograph}.

For orientation, the code includes a Scryfall card importer, ontology terms for mechanics, abilities, archetypes and combos, and graph-analysis and retrieval paths. These are candidate ingredients for relational evidence in the style of GraphRAG \citep{edge2024graphrag}; their presence does not establish a fully populated graph, live agent retrieval, or correct answers to a particular strategic question.

\subsection{Playing to build the graph}
\label{sec:playtograph}

The knowledge graph is usually treated as a fixed input: someone curates it, the agent reads it. We are interested in the other direction as well, because an agent that plays thousands of games observes something a curator cannot --- which cards actually co-occur in positions that get won, against which opponents, at which points on the curve.

The proposed loop has four steps: agents emit structured traces; post-processing derives candidate relations; an extension layer stores attributable induced assertions; and later agents use them as priors. The implemented native prototype is narrower: it extracts repeated public battlefield co-presence from training trajectories and evaluates graph-conditioned models offline. It does not automatically write answer/threat claims into a live graph, expose them to subsequent agents as shared memory, or establish that a co-occurrence caused a win.

Two design commitments keep this from degenerating.

\paragraph{The extension layer is typed apart.} The intended representation separates reified induced evidence from curated \texttt{synergisesWith} relations and associates it with its generating activity. Filtering on epistemic status separates sources; it does not certify that a curated assertion is correct. Co-occurrence statistics are defeasible and may reflect the sampled deck pool, making source separation important for audit.

\paragraph{Promotion is not automatic.} Nothing is reclassified from induced to curated without a human. We regard the promotion gate --- what support threshold, what handling of disagreement between runs \citep{ehrenmuller2026sock} --- as an open problem rather than a solved one, and say so in \Cref{sec:limits}.

\paragraph{Agent reasoning vocabulary.} The ontology can represent a decision as a \texttt{prov:Activity} with objective, belief and policy information. Populating that vocabulary from all agents and answering questions such as ``why did seat~0 attack?'' against an operational graph remain future work. The native runner currently records picker-dependent traces, not a complete causal explanation \citep{sabou2026neurosymbolic}.

\paragraph{Collective intelligence, stated carefully.} The attractive reading is that heterogeneous agents accumulate shared strategic memory across runs, so that later agents inherit what earlier ones discovered without retraining. The framework supports that reading structurally. We have not yet demonstrated it: showing that graph enrichment from run $n$ measurably improves agents in run $n{+}1$, and is not merely overfitting to a fixed deck pool, is the experiment that would substantiate the claim, and it is not among the results reported here.

\subsection{World model}
\label{sec:wm}
Let $o_t$ be the perspective-filtered observation at decision step $t$ and $g$ the player's KG context (deck, archetype tag, known opponent information). The world model decomposes as:
\begin{align}
z_t       &= E_\phi(o_t,g_t)            && \text{(observation encoder)} \\
g_t       &= \mathrm{Enc}_{\mathrm{KG}}(g,o_t) && \text{(symbolic context encoder)} \\
z_{t+1}^{M},h_{t+1} &\sim M(z_t,a_t,h_t) && \text{(recurrent stochastic dynamics)} \\
\hat z_{t+1} &= f_\theta(z_t,a_t)    && \text{(JEPA predictor head)} \\
a_t       &= C(z_t, g_t, h_t)            && \text{(controller).}
\end{align}
$E_\phi$ produces a 256-dimensional representation of the visible position and optional symbolic context. The JEPA objective is \eqref{eq:jepa-loss}: next-embedding prediction plus encoder regularisation against a standard normal distribution. The recurrent mixture-density dynamics, reward head and controller are separate from that predictor; their use in dream search does not imply an already-qualified JEPA-driven planner (\Cref{sec:jepa-foundation}).

The prediction residual $\|\hat z_{t+1}-z_{t+1}\|_2$ measures disagreement in the chosen representation. It may flag unfamiliar transitions for analysis, but does not distinguish model error, hidden-state uncertainty and useful tactical novelty by itself. In particular, it is not an intrinsic reward in the belief-space KL objective. The encoder also supports a separate feature-reconstruction training path; this does not make reconstruction of a complete successor state part of the JEPA loss.

\subsection{Combining selectors with a planner}
The intended integration separates \emph{what is a plausible default action} from \emph{what its predicted consequences are}. Heuristic, graph or model preferences can supply $q_0(a\mid b_t)$; a transition model and an outcome cost supply the planning component of \eqref{eq:klcontrol}. The earlier weighted-fusion policy is retained as a separate baseline, not identified with the KL controller. Language-model scores must be calibrated and traced if used as probabilities, and a deck's preferred curve is a strategic heuristic, not a hard legality constraint.

\paragraph{Illustrative trade-off.} Suppose a prior gives probabilities $0.6$ to countering a spell and $0.4$ to passing. Their deterministic one-step deviation costs are $-\log0.6\approx0.51$ and $-\log0.4\approx0.92$. If predicted outcome costs are $0.8$ and $0$, the totals are approximately $1.31$ and $0.92$: the predicted advantage of passing overrides the prior's preference for countering. These invented numbers illustrate the decomposition, not model outputs from a recorded game. Obtaining reliable outcome forecasts and context-conditioned priors is the experiment, rather than a consequence of writing down the objective.

\subsection{Self-play training loop}
\label{sec:selfplay}
We use a champion-vs-challenger promotion scheme:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Champion-vs-Challenger Self-Play (Phase A.5).}
\label{alg:promotion}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Initialise pool $\mathcal{P}$ with $K$ randomly-seeded agents.
\State Pick champion $c^\star \gets \argmax_{c \in \mathcal{P}} \mathrm{ELO}(c)$.
\For{round $= 1, \ldots, R$}
  \State Sample challenger $c \sim \mathcal{P} \setminus \{c^\star\}$.
  \State Play $N$ games $c$ vs $c^\star$, collect trajectories $\tau$.
  \State Update $\mathrm{ELO}$ for both; train all pool members on $\tau$.
  \State $\mathrm{wr} \gets$ challenger win rate over the $N$ games.
  \If{$\mathrm{wr} \ge \theta$}
    \State Promote: $c^\star \gets c$; bump ELO by $+50$, demote former by $-20$.
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
$\theta$ is the promotion threshold (default $0.55$). The loop is implemented in \path{src/training/rl_trainer.py}; \path{scripts/train_pipeline.py} is the execution entry point, not evidence of a qualified end-to-end native promotion experiment.

\subsection{Agent zoo: algorithmic specification}
\label{sec:agentzoo}
The framework combines Python agents satisfying \texttt{decide\_action(state, legal\_actions) $\to$ action} with native \texttt{phase-rs} pickers selecting an index into the engine's legal-action list. They span the spectrum from combinatorial baselines to the V+M+C+JEPA+KG+LLM fusion of \Cref{sec:wm}; the native pickers additionally expose KL control and decision-tuned language models. We summarise their algorithmic roles below; implementation detail belongs in the companion technical report.

\paragraph{Random policies ($\pi^{\mathrm{rand}}$).}
The native-wire baseline used in the experiments samples uniformly from the
supplied legal-action list using an instance-local seeded RNG. It does not
evaluate cards, targets or future consequences; if concession is offered,
it is part of that sampling domain. The separate translated Python random
agent is a different policy: a weighted sampler with
$w_{\mathrm{land}}=25$, $w_{\mathrm{cast}}=10$ (30 for command-zone casts),
$w_{\mathrm{atk}}=8$ and $w_{\mathrm{pass}}=1$, using a process-global RNG.
Results for these two implementations must not be pooled under one label.

\paragraph{Heuristic ($\pi^{\mathrm{heur}}$).} Deterministic priority cascade
\begin{equation*}
a^\star = \argmax_{a \in \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{legal}}} \; \mathrm{prio}(a),
\qquad
\mathrm{prio}: \begin{cases}
  \textsc{play\_land} \mapsto 100 \\
  \textsc{cast\_spell} \mapsto 90 \\
  \textsc{declare\_attackers} \mapsto 80 \\
  \textsc{declare\_blockers} \mapsto 70 \\
  \textsc{activate\_ability} \mapsto 60 \\
  \textsc{pass} \mapsto 1 \\
  \textsc{concede} \mapsto 0,
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
with three tactical refinements: (i) counter-spell discipline filters \texttt{counter target} casts unless an opponent spell tops the stack, (ii) when \texttt{prefer\_aggressive} is set the agent picks an attack at the top tier, (iii) on the blocking step it chump-blocks the largest unblocked attacker with the smallest blocker. Tie-breaking uses an instance-local seeded RNG. This describes the translated Python heuristic, not the native-wire baseline: the latter prefers land, cast, activate, then attack, breaks within-tier ties randomly, and otherwise passes or chooses a non-concession action. It does not rank targets or simulate combat.

\paragraph{Built-in opponent: \texttt{phase-ai}.}
The upstream engine includes a distinct decision system \citep{phase2026engine},
not a renamed version of either Python baseline. It constructs legal
candidates, applies tactical policies and safety/loop guards, and scores
positions using life, board material, hand resources, zone quality and
synergy. Its turn-dependent evaluation weights are distributed as fitted
parameters; our experiments neither retrain nor independently validate the
upstream fitting procedure. Difficulty controls stochastic selection,
tactical lookahead and search. The \texttt{VeryEasy} preset used here disables
tree search but retains scored tactical decisions and softmax selection
with temperature 4; it is \emph{not uniform random}. For comparison,
the native \texttt{Medium} preset enables beam-plus-rollout search with
depth 2, a 24-node ceiling, branching cap 5 and one depth-1 rollout sample,
with a 1.5-second interactive search deadline. \texttt{Hard} lowers the
temperature to 0.5 and searches to depth 3 with 48 nodes and depth-2
rollouts against a threat-weighted opponent reply model; \texttt{VeryHard}
and \texttt{cEDH} raise the node ceiling to 64 and 96 and draw two rollout
samples. Special decisions may take
deterministic policy paths rather than the generic softmax path.

An important asymmetry concerns information, not just compute.
The native AI receives the internal game state; the shipped search presets
set hidden-zone determinization samples to zero and use the unsampled state.
Our external policies receive perspective-filtered observations.
Consequently native-AI performance is an \emph{anchor under its declared
information regime}, not a fair partial-information algorithm ranking.
Matched-information comparisons require masking and independently checking
the native opponent's access. The opponent is held fixed during a learning
experiment and does not update from our gameplay traces.

\paragraph{KG-Heuristic ($\pi^{\mathrm{KG\!+\!heur}}$).} Inherits $\pi^{\mathrm{heur}}$ but, when more than one \textsc{cast\_spell} is legal, re-ranks them through the knowledge graph. Let $H$ be the agent's hand and $B$ its battlefield. For each candidate cast $c$,
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{score}_{\mathrm{KG}}(c) \;=\; 10\cdot \mathbf{1}\!\big[c \in \mathrm{NearComboMissingPieces}(H \cup B)\big] \;+\!\!\sum_{p \in B}\!\!\mathrm{strength}\big(\mathrm{KG\text{-}edge}(c, p)\big),
\end{equation*}
where the indicator is computed by a single Cypher round-trip
(\texttt{kg.detect\_near\_combos}) and synergy strength comes from the
\texttt{HAS\_SYNERGY} edges of the knowledge graph. The best-scoring cast
replaces the cast subset in the legal-action list and the parent
priority logic resumes, so \textsc{play\_land} can still beat
\textsc{cast\_spell}. On any KG exception the agent latches
\texttt{kg\_disabled = True} and silently degrades to $\pi^{\mathrm{heur}}$.

\paragraph{Ollama LLM ($\pi^{\mathrm{LLM}}$).} The LLM is consulted as a discrete posterior over the legal-action set, never as a free-form generator. Concretely, for each decision we serialise the board state $s_t$ (life totals, hand sizes, lands, creatures) and an enumerated list $\{(i, a_i)\}_{i=0}^{|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{legal}}|-1}$, prompt the model to ``respond with ONLY the option number'', parse an integer $\hat\imath$ from the reply, and return $a_{\hat\imath}$. Formally,
\begin{equation*}
\hat\imath = \pi_{\mathrm{LLM}}\!\big(\mathrm{prompt}_t\big),\quad
a^\star = a_{\hat\imath}\quad\text{if }0\leq\hat\imath<|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{legal}}|.
\end{equation*}
Only an in-range index reaches the engine; invalid replies are not clipped
to a legal index. Native chat selection uses temperature $0.2$, disables
thinking and caps output at 16 tokens, with a 30-second request timeout.
Single-action choices bypass inference. HTTP/connection failures, invalid
replies and substituted concessions are explicitly recorded as fallback
events in decision traces. A completed game therefore need not consist
solely of language-model decisions.

\paragraph{Decision-model deployment ($\pi^{\mathrm{dec}}$).}
\label{sec:decision-models}
The conceptual distinction from chat selection and latent dynamics is
given in \Cref{sec:decision-model-foundation}. The current deployment uses
Tev1 0.8\,B/4\,B through Ollama's structured option-selection interface.

For a filtered observation $x_t$, let $\tilde{x}_t=R(x_t)$ be its decision
context projection, $\mathcal{A}_t$ the engine-authored
legal-action set and $\mathcal{S}_t=C_k(x_t,\mathcal{A}_t)$ a candidate set,
$|\mathcal{S}_t|\leq k\leq24$. The selector supplies a categorical preference
\begin{equation*}
 p_\theta(a\mid \tilde{x}_t,\mathcal{S}_t),\qquad a\in\mathcal{S}_t,
\end{equation*}
and its selected key is mapped back to the \emph{original} engine action,
including targets and object identities. The first implementation uses
heuristic action tiers with seeded tie-breaking, excludes concessions
when alternatives exist, and retains a pass option if present. A forced
choice bypasses inference. The 24-option cap follows the model's reported
training range, rather than implying that MTG's action space has that size.
We log the candidate indices, model probabilities and
$|\mathcal{S}_t|/|\mathcal{A}_t^{\mathrm{eligible}}|$.
This fraction measures \emph{action coverage}, not recall of the best move.
Inference failures are explicit incomplete runs; no random-policy fallback
is credited to the decision model.

Input budgeting is also an interface constraint. Local Ollama 0.35.1
rejects request bodies above 64\,KiB. The installed Tev1 tag initially
sets its context parameter to 2,050 tokens; request-level overrides do
not change that setting in the structured endpoint. An isolated derived
tag retains the same model weights and sets the context to 8,192 tokens.
A 2,906-token structured request succeeds with this tag without truncation.
This is a deployment-configuration change, not additional model training;
extended-context decision quality must be evaluated.
Raw engine snapshots exceed the body limit. Our versioned
card-aware projection retains visible identities and instance IDs,
public characteristics, life/mana and decision state, grouping equivalent
card rows without merging instance identities. It omits hidden placeholders,
library order, internal journals and compiled rule syntax trees; it is
not a lossless rules-state encoding. Projection version and request bytes
are recorded, and oversize inputs fail explicitly rather than being
silently cropped. The configured context remains finite; larger positions
can still fail explicitly. Context configurations and failure accounting
must be held fixed within a comparison.

This component can replace a chat selector at the action boundary without
replacing the rules engine, the world model or KL planning. Using it as a
critic over world-model candidates, or as a learned passive prior, is a
separate proposed integration. Our hypothesis is improved selection
reliability and a favourable strength--latency trade-off, not that
decision tuning already makes Tev1 a stronger MTG player.

\paragraph{Decision 2.0: a wider categorical action domain.}
The Decision 2.0 family \citep{vllmsr2026decision2} provides a distinct
structured classifier interface, not a chat completion model. Its released
choice contract accepts 2--255 options; Eos 0.8B specifies a 16,384-token
input budget, while Kai 0.6B specifies 8,192 tokens. These limits widen the
offered decision domain but do not remove MTG's combinatorial action space:
larger legal sets still require a declared candidate-selection rule.
The adapter reuses perspective filtering, card-aware projection, original
action identities and probability validation, while allowing the wider
option cap. Over-budget inputs and model errors remain explicit failures.

The reference interface is local Transformers \texttt{system\_one}, with a
pinned weight and custom-code revision; a vLLM inference server is not
required merely because the publishing organisation is named vLLM Semantic
Router. The classifier head must be retained: converting the backbone alone
to a chat model does not reproduce this decision interface. Choice-set
ablations at 24, 64, 128 and 255 options should report coverage, context
budget, latency and order sensitivity separately. Generation-to-generation
comparisons must also account for precision and hardware: the existing
Tev1 deployment uses Q8 weights, whereas this reference CPU runtime uses
FP32. Publisher GPU latencies are not measurements of our CPU deployment,
and released classification scores are not MTG playing-strength results.

Real Eos loading currently fails on the Windows evaluation host before any
choice is made: the upstream checkpoint fingerprint serializes relative
paths with platform-specific separators, whereas the published identity
uses slash-separated keys. Every actual model/tokenizer file hash matches
the manifest, and canonical slash-key serialization reproduces its identity.
The integrity check is not disabled. A separate local Linux CPU check loads
the unchanged package with Torch 2.11.0 and Transformers 5.17.0, four threads
and a 6\,GiB memory limit. Cold preparation takes 53.7\,s; 30- and 255-option
requests return original option keys and complete finite normalized
probability maps, using 1,136 and 9,771 input tokens respectively, without
generated text or truncation. Single-request forward times are 12.2 and
136.1\,s, not a latency benchmark or publisher GPU timing.
The synthetic fixture asks for the largest JSON-encoded amount, but neither
output selects that maximum. Interface validity is therefore not decision
quality. We subsequently load the same pinned Eos 0.8B package unchanged in a
Linux CUDA container and connect it to the local phase-rs server. With a
two-candidate shortlist, it makes 64 classifier choices and completes a
rules-terminal 45-turn loss after 406 valid actions without picker errors.
This establishes minimal interface and terminal-game feasibility, not
playing strength, larger-candidate viability or direct Windows portability.

\paragraph{Liquid d1: encoder-native decisions.}
Liquid d1 provides another non-generative option-selector family
\citep{liquidai2026d1}. The d1-omni-600M and d1-3B releases expose a custom
\texttt{system\_one} head that returns typed choice probabilities in one
forward pass with zero output tokens; their declared text budgets are 16,384
and 32,768 tokens, respectively. We pin both weights and remote inference code
and map named option keys back to original engine actions using the same
perspective-safe context and shortlist contract. This is not ordinary
chat-completion serving: a stock OpenAI-compatible vLLM generation endpoint
does not expose the released classifier head. Generative Llama/Qwen controls
may use such a local endpoint, but d1 runs through its native Transformers
interface unless a classifier-aware server is independently validated.

A pinned d1-omni-600M CPU interface smoke returned a complete normalized
two-action distribution with 197 input and zero output tokens. Its first
cached load plus decision took 51.1\,s and a second warm decision 0.52\,s on
the local CPU environment. On the RTX 3060, the same 600M model with 128
candidates completes a 29-turn game after 32 model invocations. The pinned
3B model also returns valid CUDA decisions: a 128-candidate run times out
after 701 model invocations, a 24-candidate run encounters CUDA
out-of-memory after 23, and a two-candidate run completes a 25-turn game
after 36 model invocations. These are execution and terminal-feasibility
observations, not calibrated strength comparisons.

A separate vLLM 0.31.0 deployment serves generative Qwen3-0.6B with an
8,192-token context and 0.85 GPU-memory reservation. One Burn--Tron episode
times out after 240 accepted model choices; a Burn mirror completes at turn
14 after 10 model choices and 140 valid actions. This confirms minimal
terminal feasibility but also exposes inference-throughput and matchup
sensitivity absent from the native classifier paths.

\paragraph{Active Inference / KL control ($\pi^{\mathrm{KL}}$).} Maintains a categorical belief $b_t$ over hidden-information hypotheses about the opponent's hand (\texttt{holds\_removal}, \texttt{holds\_counterspell}, \texttt{holds\_threat}, \texttt{holds\_nothing}). Control costs are charged against a passive prior, while analytic forecasts supply outcome costs. Two planners are available: a belief-tree reference planner and a sampled MPPI-style approximation. Current sampled continuations use averaged stage potentials and soft aggregation over the agent's own sampled continuations, with hidden hypotheses averaged under the belief; this is not an exact calculation of the full trajectory objective. Defaults are $H=3$, $K=48$ and selection precision $\alpha=4$ (called \texttt{beta} in configuration). Belief entropy is reported for diagnostics and is not an additive reward; this excludes that particular bonus mechanism, not every possible model-induced failure.

\paragraph{Preference potential.} Goals use $V(\eta)=-\log p_{\mathrm{pref}}(\eta)$ from \eqref{eq:potential}, with costs for life/material differences, turn pressure and terminal outcomes. These coordinates make the goal specification inspectable, but its hand-set weights and incomplete state projection may still misvalue a position. Anchoring goals to game consequences avoids explicitly rewarding sensor readings; it is not a universal correctness guarantee.

\paragraph{World-Model agent ($\pi^{\mathrm{WM}}$).} Supports the V+M+C+JEPA design of \Cref{sec:wm}. With a qualified graph encoder, the intended observation representation is
\begin{equation*}
z_t = E_\phi(o_t,g_t),\quad
g_t = \mathrm{Enc}_{\mathrm{KG}}(g,o_t),\quad
h_t\text{ retains recurrent history}.
\end{equation*}
Two action-selection modes are available: a fast direct policy over the supplied legal actions, and dream search that compares predicted cumulative rewards from recurrent latent continuations, as in \eqref{eq:wm-rollout}. The native feasibility configuration supplies card embeddings and an explicit checkpoint, but not live relational retrieval. The JEPA head is trained separately; selecting a JEPA-trained checkpoint does not establish that this search unrolls the JEPA predictor or that its recurrent dynamics are trained. Imagined continuations are approximate and do not receive fresh engine legality checks.

\paragraph{LLM-Fusion agent ($\pi^{\mathrm{fuse}}$).} A weighted-fusion baseline combining four scalar signals per legal action,
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{score}(a) \;=\; \alpha_h\,s_{\mathrm{heur}}(a) \;+\; \alpha_g\,s_{\mathrm{KG}}(a) \;+\; \alpha_w\,s_{\mathrm{WM}}(a) \;+\; \alpha_\ell\,\log p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(a),
\quad a^\star = \argmax_a \mathrm{score}(a),
\end{equation*}
with default weights $(\alpha_h, \alpha_g, \alpha_w, \alpha_\ell) = (0.15, 0.15, 0.40, 0.30)$. The LLM signal is skipped when one action dominates the heuristic-plus-WM signal by more than a threshold of $0.8$. The skipped-call rate must be measured, not assumed. Setting a component's weight to zero defines a removal intervention only if the full condition actually activates that component; the native fusion feasibility run does not supply graph retrieval.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{LLM-Fusion decision (one priority window).}
\label{alg:fusion}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require state $s_t$, legal actions $\mathcal{A}$, world model $(\mathrm{Enc}_V, M, C)$, KG $\mathcal{G}$, opponent model $b_t$, weights $(\alpha_h, \alpha_g, \alpha_w, \alpha_\ell)$
\State $\mathbf{s}_h \gets \mathrm{HeuristicScores}(s_t, \mathcal{A})$
\State $\mathbf{s}_g \gets \mathrm{KGScores}(s_t, \mathcal{A})$ \Comment{combo + synergy bonuses}
\State $z_t \gets \mathrm{Enc}_V(s_t)$;\quad $\mathbf{s}_w \gets \mathrm{DreamSearch}(z_t, h_t, \mathcal{A}; K, D)$
\If{$\mathrm{spread}(\mathbf{s}_h + \mathbf{s}_w) > \tau_{\mathrm{obvious}}$}
   \State $\mathbf{s}_\ell \gets \mathbf{0}$ \Comment{skip the LLM call}
\Else
   \State $\mathbf{s}_\ell \gets \log p_{\mathrm{LLM}}(\cdot \mid \mathrm{prompt}(s_t, \mathcal{A}))$
\EndIf
\State \Return $a^\star \gets \mathcal{A}\big[\argmax_i (\alpha_h \mathbf{s}_{h,i} + \alpha_g \mathbf{s}_{g,i} + \alpha_w \mathbf{s}_{w,i} + \alpha_\ell \mathbf{s}_{\ell,i})\big]$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Reasoning traces.} The native runner records decision events and available picker-specific reasoning, including chosen indices and explicit inference failures. Fields vary by picker; these records do not constitute a universal explanation schema or independently verify the semantics of each action.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

The scientific object of interest is the \emph{interaction} between symbolic structure and learned planning, not the package layout. The artifact supplies a native rules runtime, an ontology and card-data pipeline, trainable latent-model components, and selectors combining heuristic, model and language signals. A small native offline observation-to-graph-to-model cycle now executes, but does not qualify the full strategic-retrieval and learning-benefit study. The proposed interventions isolate their contributions after activation and calibration have been verified: MTG provides a setting for studying how symbolic priors, learned dynamics and uncertainty-aware action selection cooperate.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} The current \texttt{phase-rs} benchmark records
the repository and engine checkout revisions, the actual server identity
from the handshake, protocol version, deck fingerprint, full configuration
and per-game picker seed. Candidate selection uses a seeded Python RNG.
Engine shuffles, opponent-AI randomness and model-service inference are not
all controlled by that seed; matched picker seeds are therefore not
deterministic paired-game replays. Decklists are committed under
\texttt{data/decks/}. Engine RNG control and seat rotation remain required
for a fully paired comparison.

\section{Evaluation Design and Current Evidence}
\label{sec:eval}

We distinguish measured native execution from the controlled experiments
needed to establish learning and graph-mediated improvement. The empirical
results in this section use the earlier protocol-v106 native runtime, not
the newly pinned v374 checkout. The research
questions and ablations specify falsifiable comparisons; they are not
presented as completed learning experiments.

\subsection{Research questions and staged ablation design}
\label{sec:learning-study}

The central empirical claim to investigate is not merely that heterogeneous
agents can execute legal actions. It is that agents can improve through
experience, that different decision policies offer measurable
effectiveness--cost trade-offs, and that strategic evidence mined from play
can help later agents. We therefore separate five questions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (policy comparison):} how do qualified playing policies
  compare in outcomes, completion and inference cost under declared
  deck/opponent conditions?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (parametric learning):} does training on native gameplay
  improve held-out predictions and downstream play compared with the same
  architecture before training?
  \item \textbf{RQ3 (graph memory):} does training-induced strategic evidence
  improve a frozen consumer beyond curated relations alone?
  \item \textbf{RQ4 (complementarity):} do weight learning and graph learning
  contribute independently, or is there an interaction?
  \item \textbf{RQ5 (transfer):} can evidence from one producer policy improve
  another policy on held-out decks, and does cumulative memory generalise?
\end{enumerate}
These are planned questions, not five established contributions. A null or
harmful graph effect is a legitimate result; failure to activate the graph is
an integration failure, not a null effect.

\paragraph{Study A: policies before mechanisms.}
First qualify random/heuristic, general chat, decision-tuned, explicit-checkpoint
model-based and control stacks against an unchanged native-AI anchor.
Deployment comparisons disclose differing contexts and candidate generators;
mechanism comparisons hold those inputs fixed. A claim about decision tuning
requires a size-matched base/chat comparator, identical options and
option-order controls. A KL-versus-EFE objective claim requires matched models,
candidate sets, horizons and compute. Native-AI episodes are not an
agent-versus-agent tournament: the latter needs a separately qualified
multi-connection controller, independently filtered observations and
seat/start-player balancing. Matchup tables and strength--cost frontiers
are more informative than assuming a transitive Elo ranking.

\paragraph{Tournament reporting and Buchholz.}
In a future Swiss tournament, agents play only a subset of opponents, so
equal match points can reflect different schedule difficulty. With three
points for a win, one for a genuine draw and zero for a loss, let $P_j$
be opponent $j$'s final match points. The Buchholz tiebreak for agent $i$ is
\begin{equation}
B_i=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_i} P_{\operatorname{opp}(i,r)},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}_i$ contains its played rounds: an opponent encountered
twice contributes twice. For example, opponents finishing with 6, 9 and
12 points yield $B_i=27$. Among agents tied on their own points, a larger
Buchholz value indicates a higher-scoring opponent schedule, not a larger
win probability or an estimate of causal policy strength. Byes and forfeits
need a declared convention; operational failures are not silently scored
as genuine draws. Our proposed convention excludes unplayed rounds from
this sum and reports them separately. Balanced head-to-head outcome tables
and uncertainty remain the primary comparison; no current native Swiss
standings are claimed.

\paragraph{Study B: demonstrate learning rather than checkpoint loading.}
Separate native games into training, validation and final test partitions
before any extraction or optimisation. Compare initial weights, predictive
training, policy training and a qualified joint-training condition using
declared data/optimisation budgets. Replicate initialisation/training runs
and evaluate prespecified training milestones; select checkpoints on validation,
not the final holdout. Report both downstream game outcomes and prediction
diagnostics: reward/terminal calibration, action sensitivity, latent collapse
and multi-step error. Embedding errors from independently changing latent
spaces are not directly comparable without a common evaluation representation.
The current one-step JEPA objective and recurrent dream-search path are
distinct; a JEPA-planning ablation needs a qualified connection between
latent prediction, reward learning and action evaluation.

\paragraph{Study C: an intervention on graph memory.}
Freeze consumer weights and immutable card facts. Compare no relational
retrieval ($K_{\mathrm{none}}$), curated relations ($K_{\mathrm{cur}}$),
curated plus training-induced evidence ($K_{\mathrm{ind}}$), and curated plus
type/degree/support-matched shuffled evidence ($K_{\mathrm{shuffle}}$).
Hold non-relational card information, retrieval/token budgets and candidate
actions fixed. The principal contrast is
$K_{\mathrm{ind}}-K_{\mathrm{cur}}$; the shuffled arm controls extra graph
volume and context. More triples or winning-deck co-occurrence alone do not
demonstrate useful strategic knowledge.

Induced evidence retains generating run/game/deck, extraction version,
support/refutation and opportunity counts, with deck context to expose
co-occurrence confounding. Retrieval traces identify which evidence was
used and how it affected scoring. Blinded relation review and removal probes
support mechanism interpretation, but observed associations remain defeasible.
No test-game evidence is inserted before the locked evaluation finishes.

Instead of every possible model combination, cross initial versus trained
weights ($W_0,W_1$) with the three principal graph conditions:

For a prespecified game-level estimand $Y$, the interaction is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{int}}=

-[Y(W_0,K_{\mathrm{ind}})-Y(W_0,K_{\mathrm{cur}})].
\end{equation}
Report its uncertainty rather than declaring synergy from a high full-stack
point estimate. Subsequently remove LLM reranking, graph retrieval, trained
forecasts and planning one at a time in a qualified fusion consumer.
Fixed-weight removal and validation-retuned removal answer different questions;
equal-compute and natural deployment-cost comparisons are also separate.

\paragraph{Study E: transfer and cumulative evidence.}
Generate graphs from different producer policies under equal experience
exposure and extraction rules. A frozen consumer receives its own-policy,
another producer's, mixed-producer or curated-only graph. Evaluate held-out
deck families with explicit card/deck overlap audits; multiple burn lists
are not independent strategic archetypes. Freeze successive graph snapshots
after training-only batches, use validation to monitor accumulation, and
reserve an untouched final test for the locked final snapshot. Separate
graph-only accumulation from joint weight/graph updates and replicate
independent producer runs: successive snapshots from one run are correlated.

\paragraph{Shared validity and publication boundaries.}
Whole games, deck families and producer batches define splits; decision-row
splitting leaks both trajectories and induced relations. Deployed retrieval
must respect perspective filtering. Before strength runs, require at least
20 calibration games per intended condition, at least 95\,\% completion,
zero protocol/illegal-choice failures and audited action semantics.
Prespecify principal contrasts, samples, exclusions and stopping rules.
Report wins/completed, completion and wins/scheduled together, retaining
genuine draws separately from operational failures. Cluster uncertainty over
training/graph-producer runs as well as evaluation games and stratify by
deck/opponent; correct the confirmatory contrast family for multiplicity.
A roughly five-percentage-point two-arm effect near 50\,\% needs about 1,570
independent games per arm for 80\,\% power at two-sided 5\,\%, before
clustering or multiplicity; the factorial requires simulation-based power.
These are planning requirements, not authorisation for an unlimited campaign.

Component-activation and loaded-parameter audits are mandatory: a missing
checkpoint, random initialisation, heuristic fallback or unused graph cannot
be credited to the named full model. The core study must substantiate
policy comparison, learning and graph memory; cross-agent accumulation and
Commander are extensions only after their independent gates pass.

\paragraph{What is being tested.}
The unit of deployment is one controlled seat against a fixed
\texttt{phase-ai} difficulty, deck and rules-engine revision. We compare

\paragraph{Measurements and failure accounting.}
The implemented harness reports completed-game win rate with Wilson
95\,\% intervals, completion rate and terminal-reason counts. Genuine
engine-terminal draws are distinguished from timeouts, action caps,
rejections, AI-driver faults and inference errors. A run with no completed
games has an undefined win rate, not a zero score. Reporting the completion
denominator alongside win rate is necessary because dropping difficult
failed games can bias apparent strength. Decision traces record latency;
aggregate p50/p95 includes attempted decisions in the retained game trace,
including explicit inference failures. Candidate coverage and the number
of decisions requiring a shortlist are also reported. Forced choices and
warm/cold model starts must be separated in the eventual performance study.

\paragraph{What remains controlled experimentally.}
The pilot uses a fixed explicit deck and interleaves objective arms over
the same picker-seed schedule. Replication across held-out deck archetypes,
engine/AI seeds and seat assignments is planned after interface validation;
we do not tune and report on the same holdout. The present chat bridge
shares Tev1's card-aware filtered projection, but offers all actions and
permits explicitly traced random fallback; Tev1 uses a shortlist and surfaces
inference errors. This is a
\emph{deployment-stack} comparison until context, shortlist and fallback
accounting are matched. Shortlists of 8, 16 and 24 options, controlled
option permutations and a size-matched general-model comparator are
proposed ablations, not completed measurements. Benchmark outputs record
model identifiers and configuration; runtime versions, quantisation,
hardware and warm-up state must accompany published timing results.

\paragraph{Data freeze and Commander qualification.}
We maintain separate, versioned inputs: Scryfall oracle records and rulings
for immutable card facts and graph construction, and the pinned engine's
compiled definitions for executable mechanics. Updating one does not update
the other or retrain a policy. Evaluation must record source dates, checksums,
deck payloads, engine/policy revisions and the support status of all study
cards; graph population is validated against that same frozen snapshot.

The native pod wrapper can configure four Commander decks with one
Python-controlled host and three native AI opponents. This is not a
four-policy zoo tournament. Terminal pod qualification, elimination handling,
transport limits and seat/deck rotation are separate gates; a turn-capped
development smoke is never counted as a completed pod or draw. Multiplayer
beliefs and diplomacy are not established by running a single-opponent
latent planner in a four-player room.

In runtime-feasibility exercises with a Krenko host deck against native
Atraxa, Meren and Urza decks, the heuristic host completed three pods, all
losses; one required a transport-timeout retry. Two random-host attempts and
one Tev1-host attempt ended in WebSocket keepalive-ping timeouts at turns 55,
50 and 33, respectively. Tev1 made nine model-invoked decisions before its
incomplete episode. A further one-attempt screen covered eleven policy
configurations: three chat models, direct and dream world models, fusion,
active inference, KL control, two expected-free-energy proxies, and a
native-learned controller with an induced graph. Ten attempts were incomplete.
The only terminal outcome was one host win by the native-learned controller;
Llama and Qwen made 11 and 22 model-invoked decisions, whereas Gemma made no
model-invoked decisions and had 61 traced fallback/error decisions. The shared
keepalive symptom does not identify its cause. The single completed learned
policy episode establishes feasibility only, not a learning or graph effect,
completion reliability, or comparative policy strength. These observations do
not substitute for the prespecified Commander study.

\paragraph{Publication sequence and statistical scope.}
The executable first stage is a bounded feasibility pilot of runtime-qualified
random, heuristic and objective stacks. It checks completion and audits action
semantics before adding more arms. Decision models, matched chat rerankers,
KG induction and learned JEPA/dreaming enter strength evaluation only after
their interface, provenance and model-calibration gates pass. The standalone
ontology resource can be evaluated independently; an early agents paper must
restrict its empirical claims rather than imply that the whole proposed
architecture has been measured.

\paragraph{Current feasibility evidence and interface repairs.}

green creature aggro and blue-white control completed all 18 scheduled games:
nine ordered deck matchups with one random and one heuristic episode each.

with no incomplete cells and unchanged frozen input hashes. Four additional
source-attributed historical deck mirrors also completed. These checks
establish bounded execution across the selected fixtures, not comprehensive

Gemma's repeated subdecisions motivate a semantic prompt repair: listing only
action types made distinct \texttt{ChooseOption} alternatives, including
Cancel and Cast, indistinguishable. The revised prompt includes complete
original action JSON and the shared perspective-safe card-aware projection.
A separately frozen three-game repair check completes all three Gemma
games, with 168 total decisions, zero wins and a 116.4-second total duration,
under the same per-game and process limits. The activation audit identifies
30 successful model choices, 137 forced choices, and
one request timeout invokes the explicitly traced fallback. We do not treat
the completed games as proof of error-free inference. The repair is reported
separately rather than replacing the earlier timeout or mixing prompt
versions. Native randomness remains uncontrolled, so this is evidence of
execution, not a causal estimate of prompt quality.

The other full-context chat selectors time out in a separate 165-second
cooperative-budget check while turns continue to advance. With a declared
585-second cooperative and 600-second process limit, Llama and Qwen each
complete one terminal loss in 194.6 and 48.1 seconds respectively. Their
activation audits record 18 and six successful model choices, 140 and 133
forced choices, and six and one invalid-response fallbacks. These checks
are not pooled with the shorter-budget campaign, and do not establish
unaided LLM performance or reliable reply formatting.

The explicit model checkpoint was not trained on the native evaluation
distribution, and permissive parameter loading requires a loaded-key audit
before trained-model claims. No graph-memory intervention is active in this
campaign. The incomplete attempt remains visible rather than being removed
from the denominator or replaced by easier fallback episodes. Thus these
measurements demonstrate which stacks execute within the declared limits,
not the effects specified by RQ2--RQ5.

The analytic control model distinguishes cancellation from pending-cast
completion and interprets simple fixed-damage targets. These approximations
do not establish card-/mode-exact dynamics or a causal objective comparison.

\paragraph{Native offline learning qualification.}
A v4 development qualification records perspective-filtered numeric
observations, original legal actions, terminal rewards and controlled-seat
visible-zone provenance. Battlefield relations use the controlled seat,
including controller rather than owner for permanents; other recorded visible
zones use card ownership. Hidden hand/library identities and face-down names
are excluded. Four training games (burn and green stompy, produced by both the
wire heuristic and wire-random policy), two validation games and two held-out
blue-white control games all reach rules-terminal outcomes against a fixed
VeryEasy native-AI anchor. This anchor has privileged internal engine access;
the policy comparison is therefore an external-policy-versus-anchor execution
check, not an information-matched tournament.

The training-only induced relation is repeated controlled-seat battlefield
co-presence, with an exposed-never-co-present sample and
producer/game/deck/source provenance. It contains 13 supported pairs among 24
card nodes from 220 game-pair evidence records. These observations are
associations, not causal synergy, exhaustive non-relations or normative rules.
The separate curated graph uses Modern-legal, status-verified combo
hyperedges from Commander Spellbook \citep{commander_spellbook}: 3,841 source
hyperedges, 5,868 pairwise projections and 1,695 nodes. Original hyperedge
identifiers are retained. Absence from this curated corpus is not treated as
proof that a pair is not a combo. The combined arm merges curated and induced
evidence while preserving the source of each edge.

GraphSAGE learns 32-dimensional card context. A 32-dimensional latent model
trains an EMA-target JEPA encoder/predictor (target momentum 0.99, batch
standard-deviation floor 0.05, variance weight 10, KL weight 0.001), recurrent
latent/reward/terminal heads and a producer-imitation controller. Targets span
successive controlled decisions, including opponent activity, not engine
microsteps. All four components update in the single training seed. Training
uses two epochs and four games, so this is a pipeline qualification rather
than a learning-curve experiment. On the two-game validation split, JEPA MSE
worsens from 0.0107 initially to 0.0279 after training; non-forced imitation
accuracy is 0.364 in both models and non-forced NLL changes from 1.195 to
1.079. Trained-model test MSE is 0.0349, with no initial-model test baseline.
These diagnostics are too small and mixed to support a learning claim.

The frozen evaluation crosses initial and trained weights with five graph
conditions---none, curated, induced, curated plus induced, and a
card-identity-permuted combined graph---on two distinct Modern deck strata.
The permutation preserves embedding rows, topology and degree sequence; it is
not randomized edge rewiring. The Hardened Scales deck overlaps curated-combo
cards, but some source hyperedges include additional cards absent from that
deck. Traces confirm graph retrieval and nonzero controller-score changes in
graph-enabled arms; activation alone does not establish that retrieved evidence
is strategically relevant.

ontology-grounded decisions remain unqualified; resource validation alone does
not show that a playing policy used symbolic relations.

Confirmatory comparisons require independent learner runs, a predeclared
failure policy and sample size, and a fresh frozen holdout. Games are the
analysis unit; decisions and correlated seats are not independent replicates.
Report stratum-specific effects and uncertainty, adjust the prespecified
comparison family for multiplicity, and retain incomplete and unstarted cells
separately. A 100-game binomial cell has approximately ten-percentage-point
uncertainty near 50\,\%; it cannot resolve a five-point advantage. An
independent two-arm comparison near 50\,\% needs roughly 1,570 games per arm
for 80\,\% power at two-sided 5\,\% significance to detect that effect, before
additional dependence or multiplicity. Pilot durations inform a simulation-
based power analysis and explicit compute budget, not an automatic multi-day
run.

\subsection{Agent audit and deck-diverse Standard campaign}
\label{sec:standard-campaign}
Before a wider benchmark, we read the traces of the scripted agents and
found four defects shared across them. These defects made earlier
comparisons uninformative:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=1pt]
  \item Both pickers activated abilities in upkeep; one probe animated a
        man-land 269 times.
  \item Any-colour mana prompts were answered at random, so casts became
        unpayable.
  \item The tiered heuristic sampled \texttt{CancelCast} during targeting and
        mulliganed at random.
  \item The analytic KL model valued every attack declaration identically,
        so its arg-min tie-break never attacked (0 of 128 attack steps).
\end{itemize}
The fixes are an activation gate, cost-matched colour choice, a land-count
mulligan rule and a pessimistic visible-board combat forecast. They are
versioned (\texttt{action\_hygiene\_v2}, \texttt{analytic\_combat\_v5}), and
legacy arms reproduce the pre-fix agents. Auditing is a prerequisite for
interpreting any comparison: the fixes changed the KL arm by more than any
difference between objectives or graph conditions.

Ten MTGGoldfish Standard meta decks played a full round-robin without
mirrors against the native AI on VeryEasy: 90 ordered matchups, six arms
and three games per cell, for 1,620 games. Of these, 1,524 were scored.
The 96 exclusions were spread across all arms: 59 engine rejections of
actions the engine had itself listed as legal, and 37 action caps. Deck
effects are large; logit spans are 2.1 for our decks and 2.9 for opponent
decks. We therefore report win rates standardised over the empirical deck
mix from a ridge fractional-logistic model with arm, our-deck and
opponent-deck terms, with 400 matchup-cluster bootstrap replicates
(\Cref{tab:standard-rr}).

\paragraph{Shared-graph learning.}
The online arm writes first-visit (context, card/action) outcomes into a
shared Beta-Bernoulli graph. That graph tilts the KL reference measure,
$q(a)\propto q_0(a)\exp(\lambda\,\tilde u(a))$, and Thompson sampling
supplies parameter-level exploration that decays with evidence. In the
round-robin the online arm did not improve over its run: the first half
scored 0.333 and the second 0.323 (permutation $p=0.90$). It was also not
better than KL without the graph. A frozen evaluation used the learned
graph without updating it, a new seed and five decks, for 240 games. For
KL the frozen graph had no effect: $+0.000$ against KL alone. For the
tiered heuristic, which uses the graph only to break ties within a
priority tier, the graph learned by the KL agent raised the deck-adjusted
rate from 0.275 to 0.423. The difference was $+0.132$ [$-0.011$, $+0.239$].
This is suggestive evidence of cross-agent transfer, but not an
established effect. With about 260 learning games spread over 90 matchups,
most graph keys have few observations and their credit is confounded with
deck strength. A learning claim needs replicate graphs, matchup-conditioned
credit and a powered frozen holdout.

\paragraph{Difficulty ladder.}
Three decks played all six ordered matchups against native Easy, Medium and
Hard, with three games per cell (162 games). The win counts were:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=1pt]
  \item random: 2/18 against Easy and 0/18 against both Medium and Hard;
  \item heuristic: 7/18, 4/18 and 3/18;
  \item KL: 8/18, 6/18 and 3/18.
\end{itemize}
Adjusted for deck and difficulty, the heuristic and KL scored $+0.150$
[$+0.063$, $+0.239$] and $+0.200$ [$+0.088$, $+0.312$] above random. Both
scripted agents therefore still win some games against perfect-information
beam search, but this sample does not separate them from each other.

\subsection{Controlled objective and ontology interventions}
\paragraph{Objective ablation for the control formulation.}
\label{sec:eval-aif-ablation}
The claim of \Cref{sec:aif} --- that belief-space KL control is the right objective and the two conventional EFE factorisations are not --- is an empirical claim in our domain, and we state the experiment that tests it rather than asserting the conclusion. All three objectives are exposed by one picker interface. However, the current KL arm performs horizon planning while the legacy EFE arms use one-step scoring. The shared belief, transition model and preference potential do not remove this planning-budget confound. The implemented interleaved episode sweep is consequently a policy-stack pilot; an objective-only ablation requires a common continuation set, horizon and computational budget. Picker seeds alone also do not seed the native engine or opponent AI.

Three measurements are planned against \texttt{phase-ai} at fixed difficulty:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Win rate.} If the analysis transfers, \texttt{kl} should dominate both surrogates.
  \item \textbf{An irrelevant-information probe.} Construct matched positions with a learnable but strategically irrelevant observation, controlled action/resource costs and unchanged attainable outcome values. Compare selections with and without the epistemic bonus; include irreducible random noise as a negative control. Raw cantrip counts or an unchanged immediate action do not suffice, because draw quality, material and later contingencies may still matter.
  \item \textbf{An ambiguity-aversion probe.} Define the observation channel explicitly and separate conditional observation entropy, belief uncertainty and reward variance. Compare matched tactical choices with equal deviation costs and evaluated outcome preferences, varying only the ambiguity term. Declined attacks or open mana are descriptive statistics, not proof of a pathological entropy penalty.
\end{enumerate}
The existing legacy arms are proxy baselines, not full theoretical observation-channel implementations. A controlled result must qualify their correspondence to the equations before generalising to Active Inference. No separation would also be informative, but could reflect limited horizons, model errors or inadequate probes rather than establishing equivalence of the objectives.

\paragraph{Ontology-conditioned evaluation.}
The ontology has structural checks, authored-question term coverage and schema-level SPARQL checks \citep{neuburger2026ontology}; these do not establish that the broader discovery questions are answerable against populated data. The \emph{agent-facing} value of the symbolic layer is not yet measured. Follow-ups include a proof-based reasoning benchmark and independent consumer-authored tasks, with external comparison where feasible.

We deliberately do not claim ``state of the art'' numbers: we know of no comparable public baseline that plays full-Comprehensive-Rules MTG, and the contribution of this report is the system itself, plus the ontology and the released self-play infrastructure.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Scope.} The engine is \texttt{phase-rs} and therefore covers the Comprehensive Rules to whatever depth upstream does; our own exposure is the adapter, which currently flattens the new viewer-interaction schema into the same opaque choice list as ordinary actions and so cannot yet reason about \emph{what kind} of choice it is making. The ontology v2.0 covers the core game structure, the ability taxonomy, the strategic layer and the agent decision module, but does not yet encode every keyword from every set, and we do not extend it automatically per Standard rotation.

\paragraph{Ontology limitations.} Three are worth stating plainly. (i) The import-resolved schema has passed a local HermiT consistency/satisfiability run, but independent DOLCE modelling review and complete profile conformance remain unqualified: the unmodified external documents have OWLAPI profile violations, and a schema-plus-vocabulary run exceeded 600 seconds. Focused positive/negative object/event probes do not certify a populated graph; CR~205.3 is checked procedurally. A read-only Fuseki snapshot reproduces 14 SPARQL checks, not agent grounding or implicit reasoning. (ii) Card designs now instantiate the type, subtype and quality/region patterns, but the role/situation pattern has no population path yet --- roles are produced only by gameplay, and the trace-to-graph writer for combat situations is not implemented. (iii) Coverage is measured against our own competency questions, which is a weak test: a question set authored alongside an ontology will tend to be answerable by it. Extrinsic evaluation against an external benchmark is the honest check and is queued in \Cref{sec:future}.

\paragraph{Representation and computation.} The present representation has 256 latent dimensions, and recurrent dynamics retain a separate history state. Larger models, deeper rollouts and more samples change inference cost; whether they improve playing strength must be measured. Planning accuracy cannot be inferred from model size or one-step training loss.

\paragraph{The control formulation is adopted, not re-derived.} We use the belief-space objective motivated by \citet{kaufmann2026aifcontrol}; the contribution is its proposed domain transfer and controlled evaluation, not a new equivalence theorem. Native KL rollouts presently use an analytic approximation. A learned latent predictor is a proposed replacement, not a JEPA decoder of complete game states. The tree planner's default continuation set is narrow, while the current sampled planner uses open-loop continuations. Neither presently establishes the full contingent planning needed to demonstrate dual-control information value.

\paragraph{LLM grounding.} Selectors operate over engine-provided options rather than inventing action objects. This constrains the executable output, but does not guarantee strategically grounded reasoning. Invalid text, unavailable inference and incomplete candidate projections remain explicit failure modes. Using model preferences as a planner prior is distinct from direct option selection.

\paragraph{Decision-model limitations.}
Tev1's decision format fits the agent interface, but its training recipe is
not MTG-specific and it does not simulate consequences. A plausible choice
distribution may reflect textual or option-order bias rather than tactical
understanding. Restricting candidates can hide the best move, and action
coverage is not an optimal-action oracle. Confidence concentration is not
calibration. The model can therefore complement, but does not substantiate
or replace, the learned-dynamics and control claims of this paper.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

The controlled studies in \Cref{sec:learning-study} motivate the following
extensions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Controlled decision-model evaluation.} Complete the matched-context, matched-shortlist and size-matched comparisons of \Cref{sec:decision-benchmark}; instrument chat fallback, seed the native engine/AI and rotate seats. Then evaluate Tev1 as a critic over world-model candidates separately from its direct-selector baseline.
  \item \textbf{Native learning and component interventions.} Train and calibrate latent dynamics, reward and policy components on frozen native trajectories with independent training seeds. Evaluate the weight/graph factorial and leave-component-out fusion under activation audits, before attributing an effect to JEPA, a graph or a language model.
  \item \textbf{Deck-diversity generalisation.} A larger study should cover distinct strategic demands: tempo, midrange, control, ramp, graveyard recursion, artifact synergy, prison and multi-card combo, rather than mainly variations on burn. Include multiple independently constructed lists per family and hold out complete construction families; audit shared cards and combo packages to prevent near-duplicate leakage. Measure within-family piloting, unseen-list adaptation and unseen-playstyle transfer separately, with equal exposure across producers and per-archetype outcomes rather than only a pooled win rate. Cross-format and Commander transfer should be separate experiments because legality, starting life, deck size and multiplayer incentives also change. The present seven-list pool contains related burn variants and cannot substantiate this broad generalisation claim.
  \item \textbf{Strategy-aware opening decisions.} Compare archetype-sensitive keep/bottom policies under matched information and mulligan rules; evaluate learned forecasts only after their opening-hand calibration is established.
  \item \textbf{Commander politics.} Threat assessment across $N{>}2$ opponents, including a multi-agent extension of the active-inference objective, and the Commander variant of the mulligan (free first mulligan).
  \item \textbf{Ontology evolution and ABox migration.} Migrate the Scryfall importer onto the v2.0 role/situation and quality/region patterns so the expressive gain reaches the populated graph, then wire the framework into a human-in-the-loop ontology-evolution toolchain \citep{ontology_extender, garijo2026maseo} so the schema is updated semi-automatically per Standard rotation, with humans adjudicating debated axioms. The retrieval-scoped extension strategy of \citet{lippolis2026ontoextend} --- retrieve the relevant ontology fragment per competency question rather than stuffing the whole TBox into context --- is the approach we intend to follow.
  \item \textbf{Promotion of induced evidence.} Define and evaluate the gate by which an \texttt{mtg:InducedSynergy} with sufficient support may be promoted to a curated \texttt{synergisesWith} edge, including how to represent and surface disagreement between runs \citep{ehrenmuller2026sock}. This is the point at which the curated/induced boundary becomes a policy question rather than a schema question.
  \item \textbf{Structured interaction handling.} Consume \texttt{phase-rs}'s viewer-interaction schema natively instead of flattening it, so the agent can distinguish a targeting decision from a mode choice from a damage assignment --- each of which has a different shape of belief dependence.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}
We have presented a research framework for combining native rules execution, structured knowledge and learned planning in Magic: The Gathering. The system implements candidate components and a completion-aware native evaluation interface, but does not yet demonstrate the complete ontology/KG/JEPA learning architecture end-to-end. Current evidence consists of resource validation and bounded native execution measurements. The self-contained study design separates policy comparison, parametric learning, graph memory, their interaction and cross-agent transfer. Controlled strength comparisons, live graph grounding and multiplayer qualification remain prerequisites for empirical learning claims.

\paragraph{Reproducibility statement.} Code, the OWL ontology, sample decklists, training scripts and this report are available at \url{https://github.com/cuteredpwnda/opposition-agents-playing-mtg}. Project-authored materials use the repository's MIT license; third-party materials retain their own terms. Retained local manifests record available source, deck and checkpoint hashes and failures, but raw runs are not all publicly archived and native randomness is not yet reproducible from picker seeds.
